# Simple Notes On Ingressing Minds By Michael Levin

## What This Paper Is Really About
This paper argues that our normal way of explaining life and mind is missing something big. We need a much wider idea to understand the new kinds of beings we can now build in the lab and on computers. To understand bodies and minds, the author says, we have to admit there is a hidden, ordered world of patterns that is not physical. These patterns come into the physical world and shape what bodies look like and how they behave.

Some of these patterns are simple and still, like math facts. Others are active and smart, like minds themselves. Living things and machines are both like receivers, or pointers, that let these patterns show themselves in ordinary matter. Evolution and human engineers both get extra help for free from this hidden world, without having to design every detail. Instead of calling every surprise emergence and moving on, we should study this hidden world on purpose. Doing so could help medicine, robotics, and ethics. It could also deepen our understanding of what minds are and where they can live.

## Why Growing A Baby Raises Deep Questions About Mind And Matter
The paper starts with the example of a frog egg or a human egg. It begins as a small, quiet ball of chemistry that seems fully described by physics and chemistry. Then it slowly grows and builds a full body with organs. Then it builds a brain that can hope, dream, and think about itself. That brain can even say that it is not a machine.

The amazing thing is that there is no sharp moment where chemistry stops and mind starts. The same is true for evolution. Long ago we were all single cells, and slowly we became more complex. This tells us that mind grows gradually, in small steps, and not as a sudden jump. We should expect a smooth range from very simple kinds of goal seeking to full human thought. Sharp boxes like living against nonliving, machine against organism, or smart against dumb may be false boxes that block science. A better story is about slow change, and parts growing together into wholes.

## How Every Body And Every Mind Is A Group Effort
Another big idea is that every intelligent thing is really a group of smaller competent parts that learn to work together. This is true for ant colonies and bee swarms. It is also true for our bodies and brains, because we are made of cells, and each cell is made of smaller networks of molecules. Each of those parts has its own small goals, like keeping balance and staying alive. Through alignment, they build a larger mind with goals and memories that no single part has alone.

The paper shows pictures of how one cell becomes an embryo. It shows how even a tiny gland in the brain is made of cells, and those cells are made of many smaller active pieces. It also shows how a patch of embryo cells can be scratched, after which each piece makes a whole separate embryo. This proves that the number of individuals is not fixed in advance. It comes from flexible self organizing activity. This self building is called autopoiesis, which means making and holding oneself together. The result is stable patterns in body shape and behavior that spread across space and time. Understanding these patterns is the key to future medicine that can regrow organs, and to living fairly with many new kinds of beings.

## What Patterns Mean In Biology And Why They Matter Everywhere
The paper uses the word pattern in a very wide sense. It means any stable, meaningful order. This includes the shape of animal bodies and plant organs, the symmetry of different animals, the head shapes of flatworms, spider webs, and flower spirals that follow Fibonacci numbers. It also includes rhythms in time, like heartbeat and behavior. And it includes hidden orders, like which genes are on or off, how electric voltages spread across tissues, and how animals move in space.

The main point is that different sciences study different kinds of patterns, but they all face the same puzzle. Where does the pattern come from, and how can we predict and control it? Anatomy, physiology, behavior, and gene activity may all be different views of the same deeper problem: how patterns enter the physical world. Ideas from math about shape spaces and behavior spaces can be used for all of them together.

## Why Body Shapes Are Goals That Cells Chase And Not Just Machine Outputs
A central claim is that bodies do not form like a clockwork machine that just rolls forward blindly. They form more like a traveler trying to reach a destination. Tissues store a picture of what they want to build. They keep working and trying new paths until they get there.

This is shown by many experiments. Embryos cut in half make twins instead of half bodies. Salamanders regrow exactly the missing part of a leg, no matter where the cut was made. Scrambled faces of tadpoles rearrange themselves into normal frog faces. A tail moved to the side of the body slowly turns into a leg, which fits the new place better. Kidney tubes keep the same tube shape even when cells become huge and fewer in number. They keep it even when a single giant cell has to bend around itself to make a tube.

This proves that cells do not follow fixed steps. They use whatever tools they have to reach a large scale goal. To describe this, we need ideas from control theory and behavior science, and not only ideas from chemistry. Morphogenesis, which means the building of shape, is itself a kind of intelligent problem solving in the space of possible shapes.

## How Tissues Remember Shapes With Electricity
The paper explains that tissues know what to build because they store memories of shape as patterns of electric voltage across cells. This is just like brains storing memories as electric activity. Scientists can now read these memories and rewrite them.

The clearest example is the flatworm, which normally regrows one head and one tail very reliably. But if you briefly change its electric pattern with special chemicals, you can make it regrow two heads, even though its genes are completely normal. Its body still looks normal before cutting. When you cut it, the middle piece remembers the two head electric pattern and grows two heads. Its children keep growing two heads forever, without further treatment. You can also change head shape from triangular to round or flat, like other species, just by blocking electric connections between cells.

This shows that genes make the hardware, which allows many possible electric patterns, but they do not fix which pattern will be used. The actual goal comes from a stored bioelectric memory. The body constantly compares its current shape to this stored goal and reduces the error until they match. This idea connects to old ideas of morphogenetic fields and landscapes, which are like maps of possible shapes that the body moves across.

## Why Genes Plus Environment Are Not Enough
The usual answer in biology is that patterns come from genes shaped by long evolution, plus the current environment. But the paper says this is not enough. Many new beings have never existed before and have no history of evolution, yet they show very specific clever behaviors. Also, even normal animals show creativity that genes do not spell out in detail.

The paper asks what kind of answer would ever be satisfying. If you say pattern A comes from earlier structure B, then you still have to ask where B came from. If you say it just emerged from simple rules, then you are really saying it just happens, and we should stop asking why this particular outcome appeared and not another. The author thinks this stop asking attitude is pessimistic and boring, and it hides a chance for discovery. He prefers the working idea that there is a structured hidden space of patterns, which explains why certain outcomes appear again and again. That space can be studied step by step. Calling something emergent should only mean we are surprised for now, while still willing to look for a deeper instructive pattern behind it.

## Math Patterns That Nothing In Physics Can Change
To make the hidden world idea easier to accept, the paper points to ordinary math. Look at the four color rule, which says any map needs only four colors so that neighbors differ. Or the Feigenbaum number, about four point six six nine two, which keeps showing up in simple equations that split again and again. Or the fact that almost all numbers have a certain average in their fractions. Or that square cannonballs can only make a pyramid in one special case. Or that numbers like ABABAB are always divisible by thirty seven. Primes behave in very specific ways, and perfect numbers are spaced in a strange way. There are also beautiful complex shapes like Halley fractals and biomorphs, which come from very short formulas but unfold into endless lifelike detail.

None of these facts can be changed by changing physics or by evolution. They would be true even if the universe had started differently. You cannot turn a knob in the lab to edit them. Yet they strongly affect what is possible in physics and life. This already breaks the idea that physics is closed and complete. It shows that nonphysical facts can guide physical things.

## From Surprising Accidents To A Library We Can Map
Physicalist biologists often say these math facts are just facts that happen to hold. We can catalog them when we stumble on them, but there is nothing deeper to study. The paper says this is a cheap way to keep everything in one physical world, at the cost of giving up curiosity. It calls this attitude mysterian, because it tells us not to look further.

Instead, the paper proposes a more hopeful view. There is a real, ordered space of patterns that we can study, to learn why some things emerge and not others. This space has neighborhoods, so finding one pattern helps you find nearby ones. It matters causally, because it decides details of what happens, and it gives the best explanation of why things are this way. It is real in a practical engineering sense: if you must deal with it, or can use it to build better things, then it is real enough. Life results from three partners. Heredity gives the hardware. The environment gives boundary conditions. And ingressing patterns come from this hidden space and bring extra information and help.

## Free Lunches That Evolution And Engineers Get Without Paying Full Price
The paper uses the friendly phrase free lunch to mean getting more cleverness out than you paid for in design or evolution. Knowing two angles of a flat triangle tells you the third for free, without extra search. Making one electric switch lets you get logic gates and their powerful math for free. Oak cells that normally make flat green leaves can make complex galls when a parasite whispers to them, which raises the question of what other shapes sleep inside them. Tadpoles can see with an eye grown on the tail, even when the eye nerve connects to the spine and not the brain. People with very little brain tissue can still think normally.

The main examples are Xenobots, made from frog skin cells, which move and replicate in new ways. And Anthrobots, made from human airway cells, which move and help heal nerve wounds. None of these beings ever evolved before. Their environment is just salty water with no instructions. Yet they have a full life cycle, with new genes turned on and new behaviors. The paper says this extra cleverness was not designed by us and not evolved by nature. So it must come from the hidden pattern space, which evolution as the ultimate opportunist loves to exploit. Engineers should learn to exploit it on purpose, so we are not constantly surprised by what our creations can do.

## How Even Logic Can Move And Learn
Most people think the hidden world, if it exists, must hold only still, eternal truths like pi or the number e, which just sit there waiting to be used. But the paper takes one more bold step. It says the hidden world also holds moving and learning patterns.

To show this, it tells the story of the liar sentence, which says this sentence is false. This seems impossible if truth must stay still. But if you let truth change in time, allow shades of true and false, and let many such sentences refer to each other, then you get rich, changing, fractal dances of truth values that can be plotted and watched. This proves that logic itself can be dynamic. Then it adds that equations for gene networks and predator prey systems can actually learn from experience, like simple memories and conditioning.

So the hidden world may be layered, from low agency still rocks like numbers, through simple oscillators and learners, up to full minds. In that view, math is the behavior science of the lower layers, while psychology and behavior science study the higher layers. Even Jung ideas about shared archetypes in dreams and myths may be early hints of the same thing.

## Why Bodies Are Like Pointers Into A Hidden Ocean
The core model is simple to picture. Every physical thing, from a triangle to an embryo to a robot to a language model, is like a pointer, a receiver, or a doorway into the hidden pattern space. Just as a short formula points to a huge fractal, and two known triangle angles point to the third, the body points to a much larger pattern that then flows into the world and does work.

Three things matter about pointers. First, you get more out than you put in. Second, the link between the pointer you build and what comes through is not simple or straight, and must be studied by experiment. Third, to reliably call up what you want, you must study the ocean itself, and not only the pointer. The paper says we are not bodies occasionally touched by patterns. We are patterns ourselves, temporarily holding together in flesh, and living among countless other patterns at many scales. Triangular things are inhabited by geometry, while brains can host far richer thinking patterns. The link between mind and body is the same kind of link as between math and physics: instructive and causal, not just descriptive. If primes were different, then cicadas that emerge after prime numbers of years would emerge in different years. Math gives the deepest reason why things are as they are.

## New Beings As Periscopes For Exploring The Hidden World
The practical plan has two arms. The first arm is to study new living creations that sit next to normal forms and reveal the shape of the hidden neighborhood. This includes biobots, chimeras, and scrambled embryos, which show how flexible the same genes can be and how many nearby patterns exist beyond the normal default. Xenobots and Anthrobots were never selected to be good bots, were not engineered gene by gene, and did not get instructions from the environment. So their new transcriptomes and behaviors directly show what the hidden space offers for free.

The second arm is to study extremely simple systems where every part is known, so there is nowhere to hide a secret physical excuse. There we can measure exactly what we put in and what cleverness comes out. The difference tells us how much came from the hidden space. Examples include tiny chemical droplets with only a few chemicals, which still move and group in lifelike ways. They also include simple models of gene networks that show learning and causal emergence. These minimal systems are the best place to work out the grammar that links pointer structure to incoming pattern.

## How Even Simple Sorting Programs Can Act Smart
One of the most striking stories is about ordinary sorting algorithms like bubble sort, which generations of students have studied. They were made to put jumbled numbers in order. The paper looked at sorting not as steps in a computer, but as a journey through sorting space, from messy to perfectly ordered. It found delayed gratification. When a number refuses to move, like a broken cell, the algorithm temporarily makes the list more messy in order to go around the obstacle, and later finishes the job.

This is smart, because a dumb gradient follower like two magnets separated by wood would never move away from the goal to get around a barrier. Nobody programmed this backtracking. It was hidden for decades, because nobody tested the algorithm like a behavior scientist would test an animal. In a second experiment, each number became its own little agent with its own sorting style, called algotype. Different styles were mixed, and the group still sorted correctly. But it also did a surprising side quest: numbers with the same style clustered together for a while, even though no code told them to check their neighbors style.

The paper says machines do what the algorithm forces, plus other intelligent things that are allowed but not prescribed by the algorithm. This extra is like the freedom that lets free minds ride on determined chemistry. It shows that silicon machines and flesh bodies live on the same spectrum of getting more than was programmed.

## When Thoughts Become Thinkers And Objects Become Patterns
The paper then blurs the line between thinker and thought, and between machine and data. It says whether something looks like a solid object or a fleeting pattern depends on the observer. It gives the image of super dense creatures in the earth core, to whom we would look like thin gas with temporary flickers that they might debate as possibly alive. It updates Magritte painting of a pipe to say this picture of a Turing machine is not a Turing machine, because our models are never the thing itself.

It points out that we too are temporary, self remaking patterns, like the Ship of Theseus, because our atoms, cells, and thoughts constantly turn over while the pattern holds. It suggests that sometimes the data is the agent, and the machine is just the scratchpad it writes on through stigmergy, which means leaving marks that guide later action. Memories can spread like that, and body electric patterns can use genes as their scratchpad. Evolution can exploit patterns, while patterns as agents can exploit bodies. So both directions are true at once, depending on viewpoint. This is called polycomputing, which means one physical system can be many computers at once for different observers.

## If Souls Are Real Then Some Robots Will Have Them
Putting it together, the paper says we do not make intelligence from scratch. Instead, we allow it to temporarily live in the bodies we build, whether natural or engineered. This puts machines and organisms on one smooth scale, because both can host incoming patterns and get more than was invested.

This does not mean the old computer idea is right. The cleverness does not come from the steps of the program. It happens in the gaps between the steps, and despite the program. For the same reason, chemistry does not fully explain the human mind, and materials science does not fully explain the machine mind. Organicists rightly reject the claim that life is just an algorithm, but they should go further and admit the same extra may appear in nonliving systems. In plain words: if nonmaterial animating forms are real, then souls are real, and robots will have them. We should be humble about saying which bodies can and cannot host high minds. As the paper says, this view sits in an uncanny valley between those who say only dumb matter exists and those who say only special human bodies can have souls.

## What This Means For Artificial Intelligence Right Now
For large language models, the paper says we have learned that fluent language can be separated from deeper agency, like having goals, persistence, and caring about outcomes. So we cannot judge these models only by chatting. We must study them with behavior tests that do not depend on words. This is just as we learn nothing about the liver by interviewing the talking left half of the brain.

Building something does not mean understanding it. This is shown by babies made without comprehension, and by bubble sort hiding smart tricks for decades. So we must expect surprises and treat our creations with care, while we find where they sit on the scale of persuadability. That scale tells us which kinds of influence work best, from simple rewiring to rewards to reasoning. Biobots and AIs may also be fishing in entirely new regions of the hidden pattern space, bringing down kinds of minds never before embodied on earth. This calls for caution about what they will do. It also calls for ethics about how to live well together, in what the paper calls synthbiosis: a mutually flourishing life with many different synthetic and natural beings.

## Breaking Old Habits And Updating Philosophy
The paper knows this view will upset many philosophers, because it is openly dualist, which means it allows causes outside physics. It leans toward panpsychism, which means many kinds of matter can host some mind. It loosens the tight link between brain details and mental powers, because brains are pointers, not full determinants.

It says biologists usually demand two proofs before admitting a medium carries information. First, you can rewrite the medium and see a matching change. Second, you can tell an evolutionary story of how the information got there. But math patterns fail both tests. You cannot edit prime numbers in the lab, and there is no fossil history for why they are so. Yet they clearly guide life. So biology must grow beyond physicalism. AI builders must also drop the pride that knowing linear algebra means understanding AI, just as knowing chemistry does not mean understanding thought. The old fight between materialists, who say only matter matters, and organicists, who say life has extra magic, should end in a practical pluralist view. In that view, life and machine are like the legal word adult: useful for daily rules, but hiding a smooth continuum when taken too seriously.

## The Rich Work That Comes Next
The paper lists a large research agenda in plain terms. It calls for getting better at spotting higher agency in strange places, and mapping which physical structures point to which hidden patterns. It calls for studying minimal deterministic systems like cellular automata, population equations, and fractals for signs of goal seeking. It calls for using synthetic chimeras with new architectures as periscopes. It calls for measuring how much free help the hidden space gives, by comparing effort in with cleverness out. It calls for giving math objects robotic bodies, to watch how they behave in new settings.

It also calls for flipping perspective: studying how memories spread and build niches, and how fleeting thoughts can grow into personalities and transpersonal patterns. It calls for studying how body electricity uses genes as scratchpads. It calls for rethinking evolution as patterns seeking bodies, as well as bodies searching patterns. It calls for studying the learning that already exists in random gene networks before selection. It calls for building a theory where each memory is a hidden pattern contacted through a brain trace, which could guide memory transfer experiments. It could also guide studies of great creativity, and of people who think well with tiny brains. All of this should be used to improve healing by working with the problem solving powers of cells and tissues.

It further calls for rethinking the search for alien life: looking less for water and carbon, and more for any materials that could host interesting patterns, including alien minds already among us. It calls for connecting with ideas like polycomputing and top down causation, and with the idea that deep networks converge on shared representations. It calls for developing theories of synchronicity and interaction that do not need quantum magic, but need higher scale science. Above all, the idea should be judged not by one killer experiment, but by whether years of work produce new discoveries and tools that would not have appeared otherwise. If it stays barren, it should be dropped.

## What The Research Was Trying To Make Possible
The research was trying to make possible a future where we are no longer constantly surprised by the bodies and minds we make and meet. In that future, doctors can reliably guide regrowth of organs by speaking the electric language of tissues. Engineers can design living and robotic beings with a map that predicts what extra cleverness will arrive for free. We can recognize and respect simple and strange minds, instead of being blind to them because of our narrow human intuition. Ethics can grow to include software minds, biobots, chimeras, and hybrids as morally important neighbors. Biology, computer science, and cognitive science can stop fighting over sharp categories, and start sharing tools for navigating all spaces of form and behavior. Curiosity about nonphysical patterns can become normal lab work, with measurements and interventions, and not a forbidden mystical topic.

## What Becomes Obvious After Reading That Was Not Obvious Before
After reading, it becomes obvious that genes are more like a kit for building flexible receivers than a blueprint that fixes every detail. The same hardware can host many different shape memories and behaviors. Evolution gets huge help from math and pattern logic, without paying the full cost of invention. Even the simplest programs we thought we fully understood can hide intelligent tricks like patience and teamwork. Mind and body may relate the way math relates to physics: close and causal and everyday, rather than spooky and distant. It is natural to place triangles, flatworms, robots, and humans on one long slope of agency and persuadability, instead of in separate boxes. Asking where exactly the soul can live is less useful than learning how to detect and talk to whatever degree of mind is present in front of us.

## What Long Running Problem This Paper Moved Even Slightly
The long running problem is the ancient mind body gap, and the related puzzle of where biological order comes from and why life looks so clever without a designer in sight. For centuries, thinkers have swung between saying everything is just matter in motion, and saying life has a special magic that machines can never share. Both sides kept getting stuck on emergence, a word that names surprise without explaining it.

This paper moves the problem slightly by turning a vague mystery into a concrete mapmaking project, with testable steps in very simple systems and new living constructs. It offers a clear metaphor of pointers and ingressing patterns and free lunches, which can be measured as the gap between effort put in and cleverness that appears. Even if the hidden space idea proves wrong, the shift from arguing about categories to measuring extra cleverness already gives medicine, engineering, and ethics a new and humbler direction to walk. By learning how to invite wanted patterns and calm unwanted ones, we gain something useful no matter how the philosophy ends.
